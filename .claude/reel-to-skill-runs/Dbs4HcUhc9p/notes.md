## Kallaway: the addiction loop

Dopamine is the prediction chemical, not the pleasure chemical. It fires while you're waiting to find out. A vending machine (same result every time) isn't addictive. A slot machine (uncertain) is.

Four steps, on repeat:

1. **Stakes.** Turns the brain's prediction on. Needs three things: a character you can picture (can be "I"), something at risk, and urgency ("48 hours", "the call was in 2 hours" beats "eventually"). Stakes don't need to be life or death, just personally relevant. Test before writing: who is it happening to, what's at risk, why now.
2. **Big question.** Give just enough specific info that the viewer makes a prediction. Vague teasers ("something crazy happened", "you won't believe it") don't work, because there's nothing to predict. Before any reveal, back up and give the context that lets them guess. Load the big question early so the drip lasts longer.
3. **Head fake.** Break the prediction, but in a way that makes sense straight away (the magician: show the empty hat, then the rabbit). A random surprise just confuses. Steps: work out what the viewer will predict, then reveal something different that clicks. Bigger surprise, bigger spike.
4. **Re-hook.** Close one loop and open the next in the same breath. No dead air, no "anyway". Connecting phrases: "which would have been great, except…", "but here's the problem with that", "and that's when I realised", "which is exactly why…". Danger zones are wherever a section ends.

Example he uses: "she told me she was doubling her budget, which would have been amazing if I hadn't just signed an exclusivity agreement with her competitor the night before."

## Vinh Giang: three ingredients

Misconceptions:
- You don't need a wild life. Power comes from perspective and framing, not the event (his dog-walk story).
- Stories aren't fluff. Facts are forgotten, story details are remembered (the orange ball with a 4 on it).
- You don't need to be an extrovert. Quiet people tell great stories with structure and timing.

Ingredients:
1. **Be specific.** Use the senses: what did you see, hear, smell, taste, touch, plus emotion. "My palms were so sweaty the paper started to wrinkle" beats "I was nervous". One vivid detail is enough.
2. **Relive, don't report.** Reporting: "I walked on stage. I was nervous. I gave my talk." Reliving: "I step onto the stage and the spotlights are so bright I can't see the front row…" Switch to present tense. Let the body act it out.
3. **Share the meaning.** Punchline, then the point, then why it matters to them. "The reason I'm telling you this is because…" The link has to make sense.

Timing: with 15+ minutes, tell a story. When someone needs a quick answer, just answer.

## Will Storr: The Science of Storytelling (from reviews and summaries, not the full text)

- Stories start with a moment of **unexpected change**. The brain is built to notice change as threat or chance.
- Tension is the fear of **losing control**.
- Characters have a **sacred flaw**: a belief about how to control the world that's part of who they are, and that the story tests.
- The brain craves closing **information gaps**.
- The brain links events as **cause and effect** ("and so", "which meant").
- **Worn-out phrases** stop the brain reacting. Fresh, specific language keeps it lit up.
- Show, don't tell. Readers fill in the rest from their own lives.

Sources for the book: [Words Like Silver review](https://www.wordslikesilver.com/articles/the-science-of-storytelling-will-storr-review), [Goodreads](https://www.goodreads.com/en/book/show/43183121-the-science-of-storytelling)

## Where all three agree

- Specific beats vague, every time (Kallaway's big question, Vinh's senses, Storr's worn-out language).
- Something has to be at risk, and it has to change (stakes, unexpected change, losing control).
- Curiosity is the engine: open a question, pay it with a surprise that makes sense, open the next one.
- An ordinary life is enough. The framing and the detail do the work.
